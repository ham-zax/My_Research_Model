The financial literature supports the framework as a conditional map of amplification mechanisms, especially funding constraints, forced liquidation, and overlapping portfolios. It does not establish that the seven variables combine into a universal fragility score, that saturation forecasts market tops, or that biological analogies confer predictive validity.

Below are 16 primary sources with verified direct links. “Theory” means a result conditional on model assumptions; “empirical” means measured associations or event evidence; “causal evidence” requires additional identification beyond an observed feedback sequence.

Brunnermeier–Pedersen, Market Liquidity and Funding Liquidity — theory; A, R, I, τ. Dealer capital and margins constrain liquidity provision. Under specified conditions, reduced funding and reduced market liquidity reinforce each other through margin and loss spirals. Crucially, margins can instead stabilize prices when financiers recognize temporary liquidity discounts and expect convergence. The archived working paper explicitly models complementary buyers arriving later. This supports conditional feedback, not an unconditional rule that falling liquidity raises margins. Verified author-hosted working paper, 2005; subsequently published 2009, especially pp. 1–4.

Adrian–Shin, Liquidity and Leverage — empirical plus mechanism; A, R. Their intermediary data show procyclical marked-to-market leverage and active balance-sheet adjustment. Dealer repo changes forecast innovations in measured market risk. Association and predictive regressions do not independently identify the full causal loop. Their accounting discussion also defeats a simple depletion story: with unchanged liabilities, rising asset values increase equity and mechanically reduce leverage; active intermediaries may then expand positions and borrowing. A price rise can therefore create additional capacity before increased exposure makes subsequent losses more dangerous. New York Fed, revised 2010.

Danielsson–Shin–Zigrand, Risk Appetite and Endogenous Risk — theory; A, C, D. VaR-constrained traders generate feedback between perceived and realized risk; return correlations can emerge even when fundamental shocks are independent. This formally supports endogenous common behavior. The authors distinguish rapid information incorporation from crises sustained by participants’ responses: large price moves alone are not evidence of pathological feedback. 2009 working paper.

Coval–Stafford, Asset Fire Sales (and Purchases) in Equity Markets — empirical, relatively strong mechanism discrimination; R, C, D. Mutual funds with extreme flows adjust existing positions, creating pressure in commonly held securities. Widespread sales by funds under redemption pressure show subsequent reversal; widespread sales without the distress condition do not show the same reversal. That comparison helps distinguish temporary price pressure from information incorporation. It remains observational: fund flows, selection of sold assets, and correlated fundamentals complicate causal identification. New York Fed-hosted 2005 working paper, data 1980–2004; subsequently published 2007, especially pp. 12–15.

Greenwood–Landier–Thesmar, Vulnerable Banks — calibrated model, not clean causal estimation; A, C, R. Their fire-sale network combines holdings, assumed balance-sheet adjustment rules, and asset price impact. A bank restoring leverage after equity losses sells assets, transmitting losses to other holders even without direct contractual links. European bank holdings make the exposure calculations concrete, but estimated contagion depends on liquidation and liquidity assumptions. Common holdings are a propagation channel; raw return correlation is not sufficient evidence of it. Author-hosted 2014 draft; subsequently published 2015.

Khandani–Lo, What Happened to the Quants in August 2007? — empirical episode and explicitly stated hypothesis; C, D, A. Simulated strategy returns and fund data are consistent with rapid liquidation of similar portfolios, subsequent deleveraging, and rebound. The authors explicitly describe the originating liquidation as a hypothesis, not an identified fact. Similarity of holdings and risk rules matters more than whether a strategy is “quantitative.” This is suggestive crowding evidence, not a general crash predictor. MIT-hosted September 2007 version.

Daniel–Moskowitz, Momentum Crashes — empirical; S and important counterevidence. Momentum has substantial historical average returns but severe losses particularly during market rebounds following drawdowns and high volatility: past losers, held short, rally strongly. Thus a momentum crash need not be an exhausted bull market collapsing; it can accompany improving aggregate conditions. Their state-dependent results support conditional strategy vulnerability, not universal saturation. They also demonstrate how using future realized betas makes hedging performance look spuriously attractive. Author-hosted paper, especially sections 3.4 and 6.

Acemoglu–Ozdaglar–Tahbaz-Salehi, Systemic Risk and Stability in Financial Networks — theory; C, D. More diversified interbank liabilities improve resilience to sufficiently small shocks, but dense networks can spread sufficiently large losses more widely. This is explicit counterevidence to “more connectivity always increases fragility.” Shock magnitude and available loss absorption determine which effect dominates. Published AER paper, 2015.

Bardoscia et al., Pathways towards Instability in Financial Networks — theory plus reconstructed-network illustrations; A, C. Stability depends on a recovery-adjusted interbank leverage matrix and its largest eigenvalue, with threshold one in the specified dynamics. Multiple feedback cycles can destabilize a system even without higher individual leverage. Exposures and recovery assumptions matter; node count and connectivity alone do not determine stability. Their European network analysis reconstructs confidential exposures, adding measurement uncertainty. Spectral radius is an appropriate mathematical direction, but its financial meaning must come from the actual propagation law. Nature Communications, 2017.

Tóth et al., Anomalous Price Impact and the Critical Nature of Liquidity in Financial Markets — model plus proprietary empirical evidence; S/A distinction. Large trading programs exhibit concave, approximately square-root impact, supported by their futures executions. This concerns price movement conditional on executed order size, volume, volatility, and execution structure. It does not demonstrate that markets saturate in their response to information, nor that declining marginal impact forecasts reversals. Trading is endogenous to information and liquidity, so measured impact is not automatically an intervention effect. Author-firm paper, 2011.

Bucci et al., Slow Decay of Impact in Equity Markets — empirical; S, τ. More than eight million institutional metaorders show price relaxation after execution. Estimated decay depends on removing multiday correlation among successive trade signs; observed persistence cannot be assigned entirely to a single order. The paper separates mechanical market response from information-related response and notes uncertainty about long-run impact. This directly cautions against equating persistent order flow, persistent information, and permanent price changes. Author-firm paper, 2019.

Bank of England, December 2022 Financial Stability Report, LDI chapter — official event reconstruction; A, R, I, τ, C. Falling gilt prices caused collateral demands; insufficient usable buffers and operational delays impeded recapitalization, especially in pooled funds. Sales could reinforce price declines. Estimated margin/collateral calls exceeded £70 billion; LDI funds sold about £23 billion and DB schemes about £14 billion of gilts during 23 September–14 October. Other assets and cash also met calls. The temporary purchase facility accompanied improved functioning and deleveraging. This is strong institutional evidence of the mechanism, but concurrent policy/news changes prevent treating the episode as a randomized estimate of intervention effectiveness. Report, pp. 86–98.

CFTC–SEC, May 6, 2010 report — official transaction-level reconstruction; A, I, τ, D. High trading volume coexisted with collapsing depth and limited net inventory absorption. Simultaneous withdrawal by liquidity providers amplified the shortage. A five-second E-mini pause coincided with replenished buying and stabilization. This demonstrates why gross volume is a poor stand-in for remaining absorption capacity, and why timing coordination can help. The historical reconstruction is not a universal causal estimate of circuit breakers. Joint report, 2010, executive summary and section 1.

Aldasoro–Borio–Drehmann, Early Warning Indicators of Banking Crises: Expanding the Family — empirical prediction; R and limits of warning signals. Credit gaps and debt-service ratios contain warning information; useful horizons differ. Property-price gaps lose informativeness close to crises. The authors emphasize rare events: their common sample contains 19 crises, 12 associated with the global financial crisis. False alarms, threshold preferences, and correlated episodes matter. This supports monitoring vulnerabilities, not precise market timing or a claim that rising autocorrelation/variance universally precedes financial transitions. BIS, 2018.

Drehmann–Yetman, Why You Should Use the Hodrick–Prescott Filter—At Least to Generate Credit Gaps — comparative empirical forecasting. Across 42 economies, 1970–2017, alternatives do not outperform the baseline credit-to-GDP gap in their tested designs; real-time linear-projection gaps perform poorly. The authors accept conceptual/statistical criticisms and call all such measures proxies. Thus theoretically appealing indicators must win actual forecasting comparisons; methodological objections alone neither establish nor destroy predictive usefulness. BIS paper page, 2018.

McLeay–Radia–Thomas, Money Creation in the Modern Economy — accounting/institutional explanation; correction to R. Asset purchases transfer deposits to sellers; bank lending can create deposits, and loan repayment can destroy them. “Buying used up the market’s cash” is therefore incorrect. Aggregate money, readily transferable collateral, intermediary equity, mandate headroom, and willingness to absorb risk are distinct quantities. A shortage can be local to the actors, currency, collateral class, or deadline that matters. Bank of England, 2014.

The most defensible hypotheses to test are:

Capacity interaction: For comparable external shocks, pre-existing leverage, short funding maturity, and low usable collateral buffers predict more forced selling and larger temporary price deviations. Test interactions, not just additive correlations. Confounders: asset risk, adverse selection, anticipated shocks, and voluntary exposure reduction.

Overlap transmission: Unexpected redemptions at one holder predict price pressure and sales at other holders in proportion to predetermined overlap and inverse depth, beyond direct cash-flow news. Look for subsequent reversal and negative controls using non-overlapping assets. Shared fundamentals and endogenous ownership are central confounders.

Delay mismatch: Conditional on total resources, a longer time to mobilize collateral relative to margin deadlines predicts greater forced liquidation. LDI provides a setting; governance complexity, fund size, investor type, and collateral eligibility must be separated from delay itself.

Functional diversity: Different funding sources, reaction rules, horizons, and liabilities should reduce synchronized forced sales conditional on gross holdings. Merely counting investors, asset labels, or models is inadequate. Synchrony around genuine information can improve discovery; coordinated pauses or recapitalization can stabilize. The dangerous combination is common action plus constrained opposing capacity.

Incremental warning value: A prespecified A/R/I/τ/C/D/S model must improve out-of-sample calibration and decision value over leverage, volatility, credit gaps, debt service, valuation, and liquidity baselines. Use real-time data, locked definitions, country/episode holdouts, precision-recall as well as ROC, and economic costs. Avoid overlapping-crisis pseudo-replication and hindsight-selected thresholds.

Saturation distinction: Test whether repeated independent informational surprises have diminishing conditional responses after controlling for surprise magnitude, prior expectations, liquidity, and positioning. Square-root execution impact does not test this hypothesis. Nor does diminishing response alone imply an impending reversal.

A coherent formalization would estimate a state-dependent response matrix, with amplification measured by its dynamics and buffers entering constraints. A seven-factor verbal checklist is useful for investigation; its predictive validity remains an empirical question.
